Your systems are alive. Optimization algorithms, control loops, AI agents — autonomous actors modifying shared infrastructure in real time. They interfere through coupling paths nobody designed and nobody monitors. Your monitoring sees the symptoms. It cannot tell you why.

You can't reason your way to the cause. No observer — human or AI — can detect coupling that isn't measured. The signal doesn't exist until someone makes it exist.

Seismologists map fault lines by measuring how shockwaves propagate through rock. Doctors tap the chest and listen. Ground-penetrating radar reads backscatter from soil. The channel is hostile, the probe reveals the structure.

We're applying the same principle to live software systems.

godon embeds mathematical watermarks into autonomous optimization agents. When one agent's watermark appears in another's measurements, you've found a coupling path. One edge. Accumulate enough edges and you start mapping the connectome of your system — the hidden wiring between autonomous actors, measured empirically, emerging from the system's own dynamics.

We're working towards systems that map their own connectome. Neuroscience does this with neural tissue — stimulate one region, observe which others respond, build the wiring diagram. Nobody drew it. It was discovered through probing. We want the same for software and infrastructure.

This is built on a production-grade stack, not research scaffolding — Kubernetes-native with Helm charts, distributed control plane, guardrails, safety limits, real-time observer. Meant to run on live systems.

So far we've validated detection with six simultaneous agents on a shared microgrid. Every coupling path detected at statistical significance. Linear channels work. Nonlinear channels are the next frontier.

The direction: detection, then intensity measurement, then topology assembly, then coordination, then self-tuning. Each builds on the last. The system that maps itself eventually cultivates itself.
